How to Get Your Content Cited by AI Engines: An 8-Step Checklist

August 2026  ·  Run this on one page today — with a before-and-after

Most advice on this topic stops at "create high-quality content." Here's something you can actually run on one page in an afternoon.

One thing to understand first, because it changes what you're optimizing for: an AI answer uses more sources than it names. Plenty of brands are being used and not credited — their framing and their numbers show up in answers that credit nobody. If that's you, you're in a much better starting position than you think, and the fix is about being identifiable, not about being more relevant.

Step 1 — Find your citable blocks (or discover you have none)

The thing that gets cited isn't your page. It's a passage that states one specific thing and still makes sense when lifted out of context.

Do this: take any 80-word window from your page. Paste it into a blank doc. Read it as a stranger. Does it assert something specific and complete?

Run this on three or four windows. If none pass, that's your finding — the page has no citable unit anywhere in it, which is why it's never quoted. A 400-word page with three citable blocks beats a 3,000-word page with none.

Step 2 — Turn every heading into a question

Headings are the handles. "Our Approach" and "Why Choose Us" are handles for nothing. The question a customer would actually type is a handle.

Then put the answer in the first sentence underneath, not the third paragraph. Extraction takes the top of a block.

Step 3 — Kill every comparative adjective

"Faster," "leading," "more reliable," "industry-best." Each one either gets a number or gets deleted.

These are unquotable by construction — there's nothing in them to lift. This step alone usually cuts a page's word count noticeably and makes what's left far more usable.

Step 4 — Add a name, a date, and a basis

Three lines of text near the top, in prose a crawler can read — not in a footer graphic:

  • Who wrote it and why they'd know — a role, an affiliation, a stated involvement. This isn't about prestige credentials; it's about there being a "who" at all
  • When — undated content is a liability on fast-moving topics
  • What period or scope it covers — "as of mid-2026," "across the 40 accounts we reviewed." This lets a citation carry its own caveat, which makes it safer to use

Anonymous, undated content can be useful to an engine and still never worth pointing a user toward. This step is cheap and it's the one most often skipped.

Step 5 — Make at least one claim checkable

Every page should contain at least one sentence with a number, a named entity, or a defined condition in it.

Aggarwal et al. (Princeton / Georgia Tech, KDD 2024, on arXiv) tested nine content modifications against generative-engine visibility. Among the interventions that helped most were adding citations, quotations, and statistics — all of which are the same underlying property: specificity you can lift.

"Mention rate went from 20% to 65% over three weeks" is citable. "We saw significant improvement" is not.

Step 6 — Say how you know

If you're reporting anything empirical, add two or three sentences of method: sample, period, platforms, procedure.

A number without a method is present but not quotable — an engine won't lean on a figure it can't characterize. A number with a one-paragraph method becomes the thing that gets cited on that topic.

Step 7 — State a limit

Counterintuitive, consistent: say what your content doesn't cover, or where the finding might not hold.

Unhedged certainty on a contested topic reads as low reliability. An acknowledged limit reads as a reliability signal. One sentence is enough.

Step 8 — Define your own terms

Define key terms in prose the first time you use them, even when you're sure the reader knows.

Definitional questions are disproportionately likely to produce a named citation, because a definition is exactly the kind of claim a system wants to attribute rather than assert on its own authority. Whoever defines the term clearly tends to become the source for it.

Before and after

Before:

Our Approach

At Acme, we believe that modern businesses deserve better. Our industry-leading platform leverages cutting-edge technology to deliver superior results, faster. Trusted by companies worldwide, we're passionate about helping our clients succeed in an ever-changing landscape.

Nothing in that is liftable. No claim, no specific, no author, no date. It will never be cited, and it barely retrieves.

After:

How long does invoice reconciliation take with Acme?

Acme reduces invoice reconciliation from an average of 6 hours per week to under 45 minutes for finance teams processing 200–2,000 invoices monthly. The figure comes from onboarding data across 38 mid-market customers between January and June 2026. It does not hold for teams with heavy multi-currency workflows, where setup adds roughly two weeks.

— Reviewed by [name], Head of Customer Operations, July 2026

Same information, restructured. It now has a question handle, a front-loaded specific claim, a stated basis, a scope, an acknowledged limit, and an identifiable reviewer. Every one of the eight steps is visible in four sentences.

Four things that don't work

Worth naming, because they absorb real effort:

  • Adding length. Expanding a page without adding claims does nothing measurable. Length correlates with citation only because long pieces often hold more claim blocks
  • Keyword density. Retrieval is semantic. Repetition doesn't help and reads as low quality
  • Publishing more often. Affects crawl freshness, not citation likelihood
  • Rewriting for tone. The intervention teams enjoy most, and the one that moves nothing

How to check whether it worked

  1. Take the five claims you most want to be cited for. Write each as one specific sentence
  2. For each, write three questions a user would ask that your claim answers — genuinely different phrasings, not synonym swaps
  3. Run all fifteen on ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Perplexity. Save the raw text
  4. Code each result: named, used but uncredited, or absent
  5. Re-run the same fifteen after your changes. Same questions, or the comparison means nothing

Reading it: lots of used but uncredited means your content works and your identifiability doesn't — steps 4, 6, and 7. Lots of absent means the problem is earlier than citation, and this piece on how engines choose is the right diagnosis to run instead.

Common questions

How many pages should I do this to?

Start with one, verify, then do your ten highest-intent pages. Doing the whole site first is how quarters disappear with nothing to show.

Does schema markup replace any of this?

No. Markup helps a machine parse what's there; it can't create a claim that isn't. Add it after, not instead.

What if my industry can't publish numbers?

Then specificity comes from named conditions rather than figures — "for teams under 50 people," "in jurisdictions requiring X." Specific conditions are as liftable as statistics.

Will this hurt how the page reads to humans?

In our experience it improves it. Every step removes vagueness. The version people worry is "too blunt" is usually the version customers say is finally clear.
