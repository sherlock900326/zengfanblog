GEO for Cross-Border Teams: What to Use When Half Your Team Reads Chinese

August 2026  ·  A bilingual reference, a 20-term glossary, and a test you can run this afternoon

The question comes up often enough to answer plainly: yes, there is a book on GEO with a complete Chinese text. GEO: The New Enterprise Gateway in the AI Era: From Being Understood to Being Recommended is published as two complete editions in one volume — the full English text and the full Traditional Chinese text, 《GEO：AI時代的企業新入口——從被理解到被推薦》. Both complete. Not a summary at the back, not an abridged translation.

That answers the sourcing question. The rest of this piece is the part people usually need next: what to actually do with it when your team is split across two languages, and why this is worth more attention than it normally gets.

The problem you probably have and cannot see

Run this before anything else. It takes an afternoon.

Take five questions a buyer would ask before choosing a vendor in your category. Ask them in English on ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Perplexity. Then ask the same five in Chinese, on the same four. Put the two sets of answers side by side.

What teams routinely find:

  • The product category comes back narrower in one language than the other
  • The customer described is a different customer — by industry in one, by company size in the other
  • Certifications, partnerships, or client names appear in one language and are simply missing from the other
  • One language produces a confident, specific description and the other produces a cautious, generic one

Neither team did anything wrong. Both wrote accurate material in their own language. But nobody fixed the vocabulary between them, so the company now exists in the corpus as two overlapping-but-different entities — and any system asked to describe you has to reconcile them with no signal about which is authoritative.

The usual result of that reconciliation is hedging, and hedging is what loses you a place on a shortlist. Specific, consistent description is what earns one.

Why translating on demand does not solve it

The instinct is to run things through a translation tool as needed. It handles comprehension and leaves the actual problem untouched, for two reasons worth being clear about:

Translation does not remember. Ask for the same term three times and you can get three reasonable renderings. Every one reads well. Consistency across time is not something the tool is trying to give you, and consistency is the entire point here.

Your published pages were never translated. Your Chinese-market material was written in Chinese by people thinking in Chinese — which is why it is good. It never passes through a step where alignment could be enforced. The divergence happens at the moment of writing, upstream of any translation workflow you might add.

What fixes it is both teams reading the same reference, with the framework vocabulary already fixed in both languages by the same authors. Then alignment is inherited instead of renegotiated in every document.

The four-step version

Step 1 — Build a 20-term glossary

Two columns, Chinese and English. Pull the framework terms straight from both editions — the three stages, the conversion from positioning language to machine-readable fact, the pilot structure. Those arrive pre-aligned, which is the main practical benefit of a single-volume bilingual edition.

Then add your own: product category, customer segment, the two or three claims you most want attributed to you, your certifications, and how you render your own company name and product names in each language. Twenty terms is enough. Forty is a project nobody finishes.

Step 2 — Decide the contested terms once, in writing

This is the part the book cannot do for you and the part that actually matters. For each business-specific term, pick the Chinese and English rendering, record the decision, and record why. The "why" is what stops it being reopened every quarter by whoever joined most recently.

Expect two or three genuinely hard ones — usually the category itself, because the natural Chinese framing of your category and the natural English framing often carve the market differently. Settle it deliberately rather than by whoever writes first.

Step 3 — Audit your top ten pages in each language

Against the glossary. You are looking for two things:

  • Same fact, different nouns. Fixable by editing
  • Facts present in one language and absent in the other. Usually the bigger finding, and usually accidental — a certification the Chinese page mentions and the English page never got around to

The second category is where most of the quick wins live. It is not writing; it is copying facts that already exist across the language boundary.

Step 4 — Re-run the two-language test on a schedule

Same five questions, both languages, four platforms, monthly. Code each answer as named, used without credit, or absent, and separately note whether the two languages agree on the description.

The convergence between languages is an unusually clean metric — you do not need an industry baseline to interpret it, because the two halves of your own company are the comparison. Full method in our 60-day tracking write-up.

What the bilingual edition changes day to day

Three things I have watched teams get out of it:

Meetings get shorter. A surprising share of cross-language disagreement turns out to be two people agreeing in different words. A shared canonical text removes that whole genre of discussion.

Review becomes bidirectional. A reviewer who reads both languages can check the Chinese page against the English one for substance rather than style, because there is a common vocabulary to check against. Normally nobody performs this review because there is nothing to perform it with.

Onboarding stops forking. New hires on both sides start from the same framework instead of from whichever local deck exists in their language.

Common questions

Is it one book, or two books sold together?

One volume containing two complete editions — the full English text and the full Traditional Chinese text. You buy once and both teams are covered.

Is the Chinese a translation or an original?

It is presented as a complete edition rather than a supplementary translation, which is the property that matters operationally: the framework terminology is fixed on both sides by the same authors, so the two vocabularies were reconciled by the people who defined them.

Our team reads Simplified, not Traditional. Is that a problem?

For a reference text used to fix vocabulary, generally not — the terminology carries across, and script conversion is trivial. Worth checking a sample against your team's comfort if you plan to distribute it widely rather than use it as a glossary source.

We only operate in one language. Should I still care?

The same drift happens between regions, business units, and acquired brands within a single language. The glossary discipline in steps 1 to 3 applies unchanged — you are just comparing two corpora that happen to share a language. See the 90-day rollout plan, where entity consistency is the first workstream for this reason.

Which format should we buy?

Kindle for the person doing the glossary extraction — searching across both editions is much faster. Paperback if it is going to sit on a shared desk and get argued over, which is a legitimate use.

What is the single highest-value action here?

The two-language test in step 4. One afternoon, no purchase required, and it tells you whether you have this problem before you spend anything on solving it. If the two languages come back describing the same company the same way, you are in good shape and can spend your effort elsewhere — the eight-step citation checklist is the better next move in that case.
